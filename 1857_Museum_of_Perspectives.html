GALLERY 1British viewpoint

Relief of Lucknow

Historical source: Thomas Jones Barker’s painting, Relief of Lucknow (1859).

During the siege of Lucknow, British forces arrived to reinforce the Residency. The textbook explains that Barker placed British commanders Campbell, Outram and Havelock at the centre, with gestures directing the viewer’s attention towards them. The damaged Residency and the dead and injured appear in the scene.

Observe the teaching illustration, then compare it with the source note. It is a simplified visual aid, not the original painting.

Curator’s question

Which interpretation is best supported by the textbook’s description of Barker’s painting?

Think deeper: What does placing a person at the centre of a picture encourage viewers to notice first?
GALLERY 2Emotion & persuasion

In Memoriam

Historical source: Joseph Noel Paton’s In Memoriam (1859).

The textbook describes English women and children gathered in a circle, appearing helpless, while rescue forces arrive in the background. The rebels are not visible. The image suggests threatened violence rather than showing it directly, encouraging viewers to imagine danger and feel anger.

The illustration below is a respectful schematic for discussing composition, not a reconstruction of the original artwork.

Curator’s question

Why can an image that suggests violence without showing the attackers still influence its audience?

Think deeper: Whose feelings are made visible? Whose actions or motives are left outside the frame?
GALLERY 3Cartoon & politics

Justice—or vengeance?

Historical source: The Punch cartoon “Justice,” published on 12 September 1857.

The textbook describes a forceful female figure representing Justice, holding a sword and shield and trampling sepoys, while Indian women and children cower. Its caption refers to the reported massacre at Cawnpore (Kanpur) and the anger and desire for revenge in England.

This teaching illustration uses symbols to invite analysis; it is not the original cartoon.

Curator’s question

What does the textbook’s analysis suggest about the cartoon’s message?

Think deeper: When a cartoon labels revenge as “justice,” what idea might the audience be encouraged to accept?
GALLERY 4Nationalist memory

Rani Lakshmi Bai: a remembered hero

Historical source: Nationalist images, poems and later popular representations of Rani Lakshmi Bai.

The textbook explains that twentieth-century nationalist imagination celebrated the revolt as a struggle against imperial rule. Rani Lakshmi Bai was often shown in armour, carrying a sword and riding a horse—a symbol of courage and resistance. Such images helped keep the revolt alive in public memory.

This is an original symbolic illustration, not a portrait of the historical Rani and not a reproduction of a specific poster.

Curator’s question

What is the strongest historical conclusion about nationalist images of Rani Lakshmi Bai?

Think deeper: How can a later image tell us about the time it was created as well as the event it remembers?

Self-check before you finish
□ I refer to evidence from at least two galleries.
□ I distinguish what an image shows from what it suggests.
□ I identify a viewpoint or intended emotion.
□ I avoid claiming that one image tells the whole story.
Teacher extension: Ask pairs to curate the same image for two different audiences. They must explain how the title, caption and chosen details change the visitor’s interpretation. Remind students that a persuasive image is still a source to question, not simply accept.

Learning outcomes

By completing this museum, students practise visual literacy, evidence-based inference, perspective-taking, source evaluation and historical argument. Teachers can use the galleries for group discussion, formative assessment or a short written response.

Suggested classroom use: 25–40 minutes. Students can work individually or in pairs. Answers and scores are handled in the browser; this page does not send student responses to a server.
